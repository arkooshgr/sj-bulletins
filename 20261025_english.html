GOD’S LAW AND GOD’S LOVE

The secret to our Sunday readings today lies in the Gospel Acclamation: “Whoever loves me will keep my word, says the Lord, and my Father will love him and we will come to him” (John 14:23). That single line from John’s Gospel gets right to the heart of today’s readings, reminding us of the intimate relationship between the law (“Keeping God’s word”) and God’s love. While human nature tends to resist burdensome rules, our readings show us the wisdom of God’s plan that guides us with the rule of law. In Exodus, the law boldly prevents injustice, protecting the most vulnerable members of society. In Thessalonians, the law elevates our behavior and saves us from “the coming wrath.” In Matthew’s Gospel, the law swaddles us in God’s compassion. In loving God, neighbor, and self, we imitate God and bring blessing to a fractured world.

Reflecting on the Word

“What does it mean to love God with all your heart, soul, and mind?” I ask the group of children who are in the midst of preparation for the sacraments of Reconciliation and First Communion. In the Catechesis of the Good Shepherd model of faith formation, Reconciliation is presented as the restoration of a relationship rather than a way to escape God’s wrath and punishment. The first moral lesson given to a child whose moral awareness is emerging is to reflect on the greatest commandment from today’s Gospel. The children respond, “We must pray…we talk about God to our friends…we must follow God…” and so on. I can see that they are trying to make sense of an idea that seems distant and abstract. Then I ask about the love of neighbor. Once we get past the literal neighborhood, the children quickly, and rather easily, recognize that our love for God is made manifest in our love of everyone. Yes, everyone. All of humanity. They are convinced that even a person who does not believe in the Christian God is capable of loving God and neighbor, thus attaining eternal life. And that believers must love non-believers—no excuses! Their vision of God’s kin-dom is expansive and radically inclusive.

The first reading shows us how closely the children are attuned to God’s desire for humanity—that we care for each other, that we look out for each other, and that we do not deprive or cheat each other. Whether it is the immigrant, the single mother, or the poor and unhoused, we are to treat them as our own siblings. Another way to look at this would be to ask ourselves: Would we oppress or afflict our family or a friend (make them suffer)? Would we wrong our sister or cousin or best friend who is a widow (refuse support and care)? Would we ask our children to pay interest on the money we lend them in their time of need (who of us with children hasn’t received that call!)? Would we deprive our friend of the sweater that keeps her warm (even if it is our favorite sweater)? The resounding “no” to these questions is the restoration of our covenant with God.
