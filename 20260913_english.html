FORGIVENESS

Nearly ten years before, a son and father had parted ways when the business they shared went bankrupt. The son blamed the father. They did not speak to each other again.

Then the father became seriously ill. The mother called the son and told him he had better come soon. The son walked sheepishly into the hospital room. The father motioned his son to him and whispered: “Did you ever think you could do anything that would keep me from loving you?”

Resentment and anger are foul things, the first reading from Sirach tells us. Remember the last things. Stop hating. Live by the commandments. As Saint Paul writes to the Romans, we are to live for the Lord and die for the Lord.

Jesus’ parable in today’s Gospel reminds us of God’s compassion. The immense sin of humanity has been forgiven and stricken from the record. We are to forgive others in the same way.

Reflecting on the Word

Peter has been given charge of the church that Jesus will build on earth, and he is told that his judgments and decisions will be upheld by Jesus in Heaven. Peter is now the apprentice leader and feels pretty good about himself. When he comes to Jesus with his question about forgiveness, he thinks he is doing rather well in forgiving seven times (the number of perfection). He is proud of his accomplishment! Jesus does not miss a beat as he comes back with the parable that gives us a new way to look at forgiveness.

I am the first to admit that forgiveness is very hard for me. I am so much better at holding a grudge. The former is an attempt to love as God loves, and the latter is fueled by anger and resentment. We may be justified in our anger and resentment. We may be suffering because of the harm done to us. But Jesus does not give us room to make excuses. It is simple and clear: we must forgive others. And ourselves.

I demand explicit remorse before, and transformation after I have offered forgiveness. I think forgiveness and anger can exist side by side as we strive for justice and hold to account those who harm others out of greed and other vices. Forgiving a transgression implies that the transgressors will work on transformation and reconciliation, unlike the servant who is forgiven a large debt by his master but refuses to forgive his fellow servant a much smaller debt. There is no transformation in this servant. I feel like that servant sometimes—forgiven, but not forgiving. I suspect many of us would relate to that servant if we were honest with ourselves.
