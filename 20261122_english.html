Reflecting on the Word

One of the moral parables that children preparing for the sacrament of Reconciliation reflect on is the parable of the Good Samaritan. A group of four children that I had been working with decided that they wanted to reflect on this parable by comparing the actions of each character with the greatest commandment (love the Lord your God…love your neighbor) and the maxims (a great truth expressed in short, pithy commands), primarily taken from the Sermon on the Mount. On the first pass, they concluded that the priest and Levite did indeed love God but not neighbor, because they served in the temple but did not help the man who was left half dead on the side of the road. As they started to talk about the Samaritan, one child stopped the group and looked over at me and said, “Didn’t Jesus say somewhere that what we do for others, we do for God?” Although we had not discussed this parable together, clearly this child had heard it and pondered it enough to remember it at this moment. The group then returned to the priest and Levite to wrestle with the question of whether or not they loved God. The children concluded that the priest and Levite could not have loved God because they did not care for the wounded man. They then went on to wrestle with how they could apply the command to love everyone in their daily lives. A few weeks later, I reflected on the same parable with adults, and that group of adults came to the conclusion that we must only help those in our immediate circle, even though, in theory, they were not willing to give the priest and Levite a pass for not helping.

I was struck by the contrast in the willingness of each group to apply the teaching to themselves. When we restrict the command to our convenience or our desires, we are not being true to the covenant that we have entered into with God and with humanity. Our waiting for the time when “God will be all in all,” when God’s being infuses all creation, cannot be a passive waiting. Today’s Gospel makes it clear that our waiting must be active, and in particular ways. In ways that serve our fellow human persons. In ways that serve the common good. In ways that serve the kin-dom of God that brings the fullness of peace among human persons. We pray the psalm with confidence that we have everything we need, so there is really no need for exploitation and oppression. And when greed makes exploitation the reality, God will step in with the shepherd’s rod and staff to protect and lift up the ones whose humanity has been stripped away and infusing them with God’s own presence of love and peace. It is this fullness of life that the Good Shepherd desires to give to each human person. It is in this embodiment of loving kindness that Jesus is King of Creation.

Excerpted from Living the Word
