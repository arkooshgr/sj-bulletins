“Repay to Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and to God what belongs to God.”

— Matthew 22:21

POWER STRUGGLE

Power struggles occur on every level of society, from the world stage to relationships at work, to our own homes. We struggle for position in the hierarchy of power: Who has power over whom? How do they wield it? What is the source of that power? What is the healthy response?

In this Sunday’s Gospel reading the Pharisees engage Jesus in a power struggle over whether Jews should show tribute to Caesar by paying taxes. Jesus’ response puts this and every struggle for power into perspective. In harmony with the words of Isaiah and Paul, Jesus teaches us that God is the ultimate source of all power—the power of earthly rulers, the Pharisees, Jesus, the Church, and the power within ourselves.

Reflecting on the Word

I was leading an OCIA (formerly known as the RCIA) class, and as we talked through Catholic moral teaching and the role of the pope and the magisterium in said teaching, the one catechumen declared that he disagreed with (at the time) Pope Francis’s position on immigration and voted accordingly. His justification was the verse that closes today’s Gospel passage: give to the emperor what belongs to the emperor and give to God what belongs to God.

To all those who use this verse to justify themselves in making certain choices resulting in the inhumane treatment of others, my question focuses on the second part of that verse. What is it that belongs to God that Jesus tells us to give to God? The passage from Isaiah says that God calls us by name, and Paul tells us that we are chosen. We belong to God. So how do we give ourselves to God? The catechumen across the table from me could not quite see that the way we offer ourselves to God and the choices we make on this journey of faith are immensely important to God. We are not allowed to evade our covenantal responsibility with a slick interpretation of Jesus’ words that serve our fears and worldly agenda. As someone who desired baptism, he intended to enter into a covenantal relationship that I knew would ask a lot from him. My job was to help him embark on the journey with curiosity and compassion, rather than political expedience. I am not sure where he stands now, but I do hope that he sees the second half of that verse from Matthew’s Gospel as being more significant to his faith than the first half.

Excerpted from Living the Word
